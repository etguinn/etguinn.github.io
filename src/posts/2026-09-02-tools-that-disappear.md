---
title: Tools That Disappear
description: The best software gets out of the way so completely you forget you're using it — and that's exactly why it's so hard to sell.
topics: [Technology]
---

There's a specific feeling when a tool is right: you stop thinking about the
tool. The pen stops being a pen. The camera stops being a camera. You are
just doing the thing.

Heidegger called this *ready-to-hand* — the hammer vanishes into the act of
hammering, and only reappears as an object when it breaks. Most software
never gets there. It insists on being noticed.

## Noticing is the tax

Every modal, every onboarding tour, every "we've updated our interface!"
banner is the tool reappearing as an object. Each one costs you the thread of
what you were doing. Individually trivial. Cumulatively, it's the whole
reason a day of work can produce nothing.

What's strange is that this is a *commercial* problem, not a design one.
Invisible tools are terrible at demonstrating their own value. A product that
disappears generates no screenshots, no engagement metrics, no surface for
upsell. The incentives point relentlessly toward a tool that reminds you it
exists.

## What the good ones share

The tools I've kept for a decade have almost nothing in common aesthetically.
But structurally:

1. **They're fast enough to think in.** Latency under roughly 100ms and the
   tool joins your train of thought. Over 500ms and you start context
   switching around it.
2. **They're boring in the middle.** The complexity lives at the edges, opt-in,
   where a novice never trips on it.
3. **They assume you'll still be here in five years.** Stable file formats,
   stable shortcuts, stable words for things.

That last one is really a moral claim dressed as a technical one. Respecting
someone's accumulated muscle memory is a way of respecting their time. Most
redesigns are a transfer of cost from the company to the user, and they're
almost never described that way.
