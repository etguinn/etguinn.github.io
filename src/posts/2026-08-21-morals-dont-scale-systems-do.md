---
title: Morals Don't Scale, Systems Do
description: Every company says it has values. Almost none has built anything that would cost it money to keep them.
topics: [Business]
---

A company's stated values are a hypothesis. The org chart, the comp plan, and
the promotion criteria are the experiment. When the two disagree, the
experiment wins — every time, without exception, no matter how good the
offsite was.

## The test is what it costs

You can learn more about what a business actually believes from a single
quarter of hard decisions than from a decade of its About page. Not because
people are cynical, but because values only become real at the point where
they're expensive. "We care about quality" means nothing until quality and
the ship date are in direct conflict and someone has to decide.

So the useful question isn't *what do you value*. It's: **what have you built
that makes the wrong thing hard to do?**

- A refund policy an agent can apply without escalating.
- A launch checklist with a line item nobody is allowed to waive.
- A severance floor written down before anyone needed it.

Each of those converts a value into friction. Friction survives leadership
changes. Intentions don't.

## Why this keeps mattering

The fashionable version of this argument says culture is downstream of
incentives, and then stops — as if that were a cynical conclusion. It isn't.
It's a design brief. If incentives determine behavior, then anyone who can
change an incentive can change behavior, and that's most of us, most of the
time, in small ways.

The people who move institutions aren't usually the ones with the best stated
ethics. They're the ones who noticed that a form had a field on it that
shouldn't, and got it removed.
